\begin{abstract}
LLM trading agents increasingly combine heterogeneous sources such as prices, on-chain statistics and news, which
often point in opposite directions. Existing agents such as CryptoTrade fuse all sources inside a single prompt and
leave disagreement to be resolved implicitly. We study whether resolving disagreement \emph{explicitly} helps: we
assign one specialist agent per source, quantify their disagreement with a KL-divergence and variance score, and,
when it is high, run a multi-round debate in which every claim must cite a source line and pass a verification step.
To isolate the effect of the architecture, we reimplement CryptoTrade, verify it against the authors' code, and run
both systems on the same data, trading environment, information timing and local LLM over the paper's bull,
sideways and bear Bitcoin periods of 2023, plus a development set of 145 unused days. The conflict-aware system trails
CryptoTrade on both the test periods (+0.38\% vs.\ +5.71\% average return) and the development set (+8.60\% vs.\
+11.95\%). Our analysis shows that (i) debate lowers the measured conflict from 0.61 to 0.22 yet changes only 11 of 195
decisions, always towards abstention; (ii) each agent's directional accuracy is near chance (45--48\%) and biased, with
the news agent calling BUY about 80\% of the time and the market agent selling into rallies because CryptoTrade's
indicator labels are inverted; (iii) on the 170 days the agents conflict, debate resolves the conflict correctly 48\% of
the time, no better than no debate, and an arbiter that trusts agents by their past record raises accuracy but lowers
return; and (iv) eight improvements tuned on the development set do not beat CryptoTrade reliably. CryptoTrade itself
is at chance at 1--7-day horizons; removing our system's SELLs closes the gap. These findings separate \emph{reducing}
disagreement from \emph{resolving} it, and show that returns here reflect market exposure more than prediction.\footnote{Code and data:
\url{https://github.com/Chivu171/Multi-Agent-Crypto}}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Cryptocurrency prices respond to signals of different nature: technical momentum, blockchain usage and news. These
signals are noisy and frequently disagree, for instance an overbought RSI alongside positive news. Large language models
(LLMs) can read numbers and text together, and a growing line of work builds trading agents on top of
them~\citep{yu2023finmem,yu2024fincon,xiao2024tradingagents,li2024cryptotrade}. CryptoTrade~\citep{li2024cryptotrade},
the reference agent for cryptocurrency, asks one LLM to analyse prices and on-chain data, another to analyse news,
and a trader LLM to read both analyses and act. When the analyses disagree, the trader resolves the conflict inside one
prompt: disagreement is never measured, explained or contested.

Work on multi-agent debate suggests a different design. Letting several LLM instances critique each other improves
factuality and reasoning~\citep{du2024debate,liang2024divergent}, and MADAM-RAG~\citep{wang2025madamrag} shows that
assigning one agent per retrieved document and letting them debate helps when documents conflict. These results come
from question answering with a single correct answer. Whether they transfer to trading, where ``correctness'' is only
revealed by the next price move and the sources are structurally different, is open.

We ask four research questions under strictly controlled conditions (same data, environment, information timing and
LLM):
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
  \item \textbf{RQ1:} Does explicit conflict handling across source-specialised agents outperform CryptoTrade's
  implicit fusion?
  \item \textbf{RQ2:} What does each source contribute, and does its contribution depend on the market regime?
  \item \textbf{RQ3:} Does debate change decisions, or does it only lower the conflict score?
  \item \textbf{RQ4:} Can improvements motivated by this analysis close the gap when their parameters are chosen on
  separate development data?
\end{itemize}

Our contributions are:
(1) a conflict-aware multi-agent trading system with a KL--variance conflict score, root-cause analysis and a
\emph{grounded} debate in which every claim cites a source line and is verified before it can change the state;
(2) a faithful reimplementation of CryptoTrade, verified to match the authors' code exactly on rule strategies and
prompts, which makes a controlled comparison possible with a local open model;
(3) an empirical study, replicated on a held-out development set, showing that explicit conflict handling trails
implicit fusion, that debate reduces disagreement without resolving it, and that individual agents are near chance and
directionally biased;
(4) a dev-then-test evaluation of eight improvements with all results reported, including the negative ones, and
evidence that accuracy and return can move in opposite directions.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{LLM trading agents.}
FinMem~\citep{yu2023finmem} equips a stock-trading agent with layered memory; FinCon~\citep{yu2024fincon} organises
agents as a manager--analyst hierarchy with verbal reinforcement; TradingAgents~\citep{xiao2024tradingagents} simulates a
trading firm in which bullish and bearish researchers argue before a trader acts. For cryptocurrency,
CryptoTrade~\citep{li2024cryptotrade} combines on-chain statistics and news with a reflection step inspired by
Reflexion~\citep{shinn2023reflexion}, and \citet{luo2025cryptomas} compare hierarchical, collaborative and debate
communication among modality-specialised agents for portfolio management. Market Regime Council~\citep{mrc2026}
weights three specialist agents online with Shapley credits and regime-dependent multipliers. Closest to us,
CGX~\citep{bajgora2026cgx} lets Bull and Bear agents debate for three rounds and trades crypto only when a meta-evaluator
judges their consensus strong enough, mainly reducing drawdown. None of these measures disagreement \emph{between data
sources} or compares against a reproduced baseline under an identical model.

\paragraph{Multi-agent debate and conflicting evidence.}
\citet{du2024debate} show that multiple LLM instances debating over rounds improve reasoning and factuality;
\citet{liang2024divergent} use opposing debaters and a judge to counter degeneration of thought; ChatEval~\citep{chan2024chateval}
applies debate to evaluation. MADAM-RAG~\citep{wang2025madamrag} assigns one agent per document and aggregates the debate
history across rounds to handle ambiguity and misinformation in retrieval. Our design follows this one-agent-per-source
pattern and adds a quantitative conflict trigger and citation verification. In simulated portfolio allocation,
\citet{huang2026debate} find that debate reasoning quality is unrelated to Sharpe and identify \emph{sycophantic
convergence}, agents abandoning independent positions, as its main failure mode.

Figure~\ref{fig:arch} shows the pipeline. For a decision at the open of day $D$, three specialists analyse their own
source; a Conflict Analyzer measures their disagreement; above a threshold it triggers root-cause analysis (RCA) and
debate; a Mediator aggregates the final opinions into a signal.

\subsection{Specialist agents}
The \emph{Market} agent reads the last seven daily opens, EMA20/50 and RSI14 of opens, and the three technical signals
CryptoTrade uses (moving-average crossover, MACD, Bollinger bands). The \emph{Financial} agent reads six on-chain
transaction statistics of day $D{-}1$ with their day-over-day change. The \emph{Sentiment} agent reads the news
articles of day $D{-}1$. Every evidence item (a metric, a news sentence) is placed on its own line so that later stages
can cite it precisely. Each agent returns a signal, a belief $(d_i, s_i)$ with direction $d_i\in\{-1,0,1\}$ and
strength $s_i\in[0,1]$, a reasoning path, and metadata. A noise score $e_i$ is computed from data rather than by the
LLM: $1-|\text{RSI}-50|/50$ for Market, the mean absolute daily change of on-chain metrics divided by 30\% (capped at
1) for Financial, and a neutral 0.5 for Sentiment since the dataset has no fear-and-greed index.

\subsection{Conflict Analyzer}
Each belief is mapped to a distribution over \{down, up\}, $P_i=(0.5-0.5x_i,\,0.5+0.5x_i)$ with $x_i=d_i s_i$, so a
weak opinion stays close to uniform. The conflict score combines the mean pairwise KL divergence~\citep{kullback1951}
over ordered pairs with the variance of the signed beliefs:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
C={}&\alpha\,\tfrac{1}{N(N-1)}\textstyle\sum_{i\neq j}D_{KL}(P_i\,\|\,P_j)\\
&+(1-\alpha)\,\mathrm{Var}(x_1,\dots,x_N),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
with $\alpha=0.6$ and $N=3$. When $C\geq\tau=0.4$, rule-based categories (signal, temporal, reliability, redundancy
conflict) are assigned and an LLM writes an RCA of 1--3 cited claims explaining the disagreement.

\subsection{Grounded debate}
In each of two rounds, agent $i$ receives a rebuttal strength
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
R_i=\mathrm{clip}\Big(&\tfrac{1}{1.5(N-1)}\textstyle\sum_{j\neq i}\big[w_b D_{\cos}\\
&+w_e D_{J}+w_l D_{L}\big],\,0,\,1\Big),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
averaging the cosine distance between signed beliefs, the Jaccard distance between evidence vocabularies and the
normalised Levenshtein distance between reasoning paths ($w_b=w_e=w_l=1/3$). Its confidence decays as
$s_i\leftarrow s_i e^{-\beta R_i}$ with $\beta=0.35$, and the agent rewrites its reasoning given the accumulated debate
history (as in MADAM-RAG's aggregator), all agents' evidence and a constraint to stay within its role. The update changes
the \emph{strength} $s_i$ but never the \emph{direction} $d_i$.

\paragraph{Citation verification.}
Every RCA and debate output consists of 1--3 claims labelled fact, inference or hypothesis, each citing an evidence id
and a line id. Code checks the schema and resolves the quoted line itself, so the model never copies numbers. A
separate LLM call then judges each claim as supported, unsupported or contradicted against the full evidence; a failed
or missing review counts as rejection. After one retry, a rejected update is rolled back entirely, including the
confidence decay, so unsupported arguments cannot move the decision. The day is still traded and flagged.

\subsection{Mediator}
Agent weights are $\omega_i=(1-e_i)(1-r_i)\,e^{-\gamma\Delta t_i}$ with redundancy $r_i$ (0.05--0.1), data age
$\Delta t_i$ in seconds and $\gamma=10^{-5}$. The aggregate $\Sfinal=\sum_i d_i s_i\omega_i$ yields BUY if
$\Sfinal>0.05$, SELL if $\Sfinal<-0.05$ and NEUTRAL otherwise. Signals map to actions $+0.5$, $-0.5$ and $0$ (buy with
half the cash, sell half the coins, hold), the sizing CryptoTrade uses for its rule baselines.

\section{Experimental Setup}

\paragraph{Data and periods.}
We use CryptoTrade's public BTC dataset: daily prices (CoinMarketCap), six on-chain statistics (Dune) and up to five
filtered news articles per day (Google News). We keep the paper's three test periods: bull (2023-10-01 to 12-01,
61 trading days, BTC +39.7\%), sideways (06-17 to 08-25, 69 days, $-$0.8\%) and bear (04-12 to 06-16, 65 days,
$-$15.6\%) as the \emph{test} set. Three ranges of the same dataset that the paper does not use form a
\emph{development} set: Dev1 (2023-02-20 to 04-11, 50 days, Buy \& Hold +21.6\%), Dev2 (08-26 to 09-30, 35 days,
+3.1\%) and Dev3 (12-02 to 2024-01-31, 60 days, +10.8\%). Every parameter of the improvements in
Section~\ref{sec:rq4} is chosen on the development set and the chosen configuration is evaluated once on test. The
development set has no sustained bear market.

\paragraph{Baseline reproduction.}
We reimplement CryptoTrade's environment, four prompts, agent loop and rule baselines. The public code differs from
the paper (it disables two of the three technical signals and reflects over three days), so we follow the paper: three
signals and seven-day reflection. Running the original code side by side, our six rule strategies match its returns and
Sharpe ratios exactly on all three periods, and therefore also match the paper's Table~2; the four prompts are
identical over 12 steps for the same action sequence. On our local model, CryptoTrade reaches +30.47\% / $-$1.63\% /
$-$11.72\% versus +26.35\% / $-$4.07\% / $-$11.72\% reported with GPT-4, so the reproduction is faithful and the model is
a capable backbone.

\paragraph{Environment and model.}
Both systems trade in CryptoTrade's environment: \$1M split evenly between cash and BTC, one action in $[-1,1]$ per
day, no shorting, 0.4\% fee plus fixed gas, execution at the open. Both use \texttt{gemma-4-26b-a4b} (4-bit QAT) served
locally by LM Studio on a MacBook M1 Pro, temperature 0, with all calls cached for exact reruns. At the open of $D$ both
systems see prices up to that open and on-chain data and news of $D{-}1$; a unit test confirms the inputs coincide with
CryptoTrade's on all 61 bull days.

\paragraph{Configurations and metrics.}
We compare CryptoTrade \emph{full} (all sources and reflection), CryptoTrade \emph{market-only} (technical signals
only), our full system, and ablations derived offline from the same run by re-aggregating saved opinions: without
debate, Market only, Market+Financial and Market+Sentiment. We report total return, Sharpe ratio~\citep{sharpe1966}
(mean over standard deviation of daily returns, as in CryptoTrade) and directional accuracy, the share of trading days
whose direction matches the open-to-open move.

\subsection{RQ1: explicit versus implicit conflict handling}
Table~\ref{tab:main} shows that our full system averages +0.38\%, above CryptoTrade market-only ($-$3.35\%) but below
CryptoTrade full (+5.71\%) and Buy \& Hold (+7.74\%). It wins only in the sideways period. The ranking repeats on the
development set: CryptoTrade full +11.95\% (Dev1/2/3: +32.33/$-$4.62/+8.13), Buy \& Hold +11.85\%, CryptoTrade
market-only +8.83\%, ours +8.60\% (+22.31/$-$2.53/+6.01). In the bull period the two systems have almost the same
directional accuracy (55.3\% vs.\ 55.1\%) yet our return is about half: CryptoTrade chooses its own position size and
typically commits 0.8--1.0 when confident, while our system trades a fixed half, and with no shorting every SELL in a
rally forfeits gains. Sizing is not the whole story, however: signal-based sizing does not help
(Section~\ref{sec:rq4}), and on Dev1 CryptoTrade is also more accurate (57.1\% vs.\ 50.0\%). Explicit conflict handling,
in its current form, does not beat implicit fusion, and this holds on two disjoint sets of dates.

These ablation differences, however, do not survive a direct per-day measurement on the development set

agent's, our final decision's and CryptoTrade's edge at 1, 3 and 7 days lies between $-2.3$ and $+1.3$ points pooled.

Debate fires on 99 of 195 days (Table~\ref{tab:debate}) and cuts the mean conflict score from about 0.61 to 0.22. It
never hurts returns. Yet it changes the final signal on only 11 days, always from BUY or SELL to NEUTRAL, and on none of
them does a wrong call become right or vice versa: the gain comes from staying out on uncertain days. The cause is by
design: debate decays the strength of contested opinions but never flips their direction, so opposing beliefs shrink
towards zero together. The conflict score falls because the agents become less certain, not because they agree.
Debate thus acts as a risk filter rather than an error-correction mechanism, a distinction that a lower conflict score
alone would hide. Lifting the restriction does not change this (Section~\ref{sec:rq4}): allowed to flip, agents almost
always restate their position.

Does resolving a conflict make the call more accurate? On the 170 days with at least one BUY and one SELL, one side is
always right, yet the current system is right 48.3\% of the time, the Mediator without debate 47.5\% and a majority vote
46.2\%. Following Financial gives 55.4\% and following Sentiment 41.0\%, on both splits (this table was computed after
the test periods had been seen). A blind audit of 30 dev debate days (90 opinions checked against their snapshots and
full articles) finds only 5 minor factual slips and none that drives a signal: most conflicts are different readings of
correct facts, so a debate that adjudicates by checking facts has little to correct.

We test six improvements (Table~\ref{tab:improve}). Three change only aggregation and are recomputed from saved
opinions: a \emph{trend filter} that blocks SELL in an up-trend and BUY in a down-trend (18 settings: MA20/50,
10/20/30-day return, 0/3/5\% band); \emph{regime weights} that multiply the Financial and Sentiment contributions by
$\{0,1,2\}$ per regime (729 combinations per trend setting); and \emph{signal sizing} $\mathrm{clip}(\Sfinal/k,-1,1)$,
$k\in\{0.1,0.2,0.3,0.5\}$. \emph{Bias correction}, also computed offline, replaces each agent's score $d_i s_i$ by its
deviation from the agent's own mean over the previous $K\in\{10,20,30\}$ days, i.e.\ whether today's view is better or
worse than usual. \emph{Longer trend context} changes the Market agent's input and needs a new LLM run: the agent also
sees the 30-day return, the distance to MA50 and the number of consecutive opens on that side of MA50.

Following the protocol, the dev winner is regime weights (+15.97\%), which falls to +0.00\% on test. Leaving one dev
range out and selecting on the other two confirms the overfitting risk of a grid of thousands of combinations on 145
days. The trend filter is the only variant above CryptoTrade on test (+9.04\%), but it was not selected: every one of its
18 settings is \emph{below} eval-v1 on dev (+1.1 to +8.5\%) and \emph{above} it on test (+2.6 to +10.6\%). Its gain is a
property of the test bull period, a long steady rally in which blocking SELLs simply holds BTC (+36.6\% vs.\ Buy \& Hold
+39.7\%), not of its parameters. Longer trend context makes the Market agent take more positions (112 vs.\ 84) without
making it more accurate (49.1\% vs.\ 47.6\%); it still calls BUY only 41\% of the time. Bias correction balances the BUY
share (Sentiment 79\%$\rightarrow$53\%, Market 42\%$\rightarrow$51\%) but leaves accuracy unchanged (final 45.5\% vs.\
45.0\%). \emph{Debiasing prompts} attack the bias at its source: the Sentiment agent also reads the headlines of the
three earlier days and is told that routine optimism is not a buy signal, and the Market agent is told to take a side
only when most indicators agree. Sentiment's BUY share falls (79\%$\rightarrow$65\%) but so does its accuracy
(45.4\%$\rightarrow$39.5\%); the Market agent abstains on 73\% of days (was 41\%), and the dev return drops to +2.47\%.
\emph{Debate may flip} lets each debating agent state a stance, which replaces its direction when the rebuttal strength
is at least 0.65 (fixed beforehand from the eval-v1 dev distribution: about 0.58 when the others agree or abstain, 0.80
when both oppose) and its new explanation passes the source check; specialist opinions and RCA are replayed from eval-v1.
Over 426 agent-rounds on 71 debate days, agents flip only 5 times, all the Financial agent moving from BUY to NEUTRAL
when alone against two SELLs. This is conformity, not correction, i.e.\ the sycophantic convergence of
\citet{huang2026debate} on real data: on 3 of those 5 days the price rose, and no flip changes the final decision. Dev return is +8.56\% (eval-v1 +8.60\%), final accuracy 45.0\% in both.

\emph{Never SELL} comes from a grid of asymmetric rules (BUY at $\Sfinal\ge0.05$, SELL only at $\Sfinal\le-k$ or never),
motivated by the environment: without short selling, a wrong SELL in a rally costs the gain on the coins sold. Dev
selects never selling; on test it reaches +7.39\%, above CryptoTrade (+5.71\%) but below Buy \& Hold (+7.74\%), and
pooled it is level with CryptoTrade (+0.48 points, $p=0.77$). The whole gap between the two systems therefore lies in
our SELLs, but closing it is a matter of holding more BTC, which is also how CryptoTrade wins (it buys on 91\% of its
directional days). The \emph{reliability arbiter} settles conflict days by each agent's record on past conflict days.
Selected on dev with records reset per window it does not help. With records carried across the year (exploratory),
accuracy on all trading days rises on both splits, from 49.6\% to 52.3\% pooled ($p\approx0.4$), while returns fall
(dev +5.33\%, test $-$1.40\%): it mostly rules against the BUY-leaning Sentiment agent and so sells more in a rising year.
Combined with the SELL rule it loses its accuracy gain. Here accuracy and return trade off.

\paragraph{Are three agents needed?} Zeroing the Financial and Sentiment contributions gives a Market-only variant that
is \emph{better} on dev (+12.32\% vs.\ +8.60\%) and worse on test ($-$1.59\% vs.\ +0.37\%). Pooled over all six ranges,
three agents with the filter reach +8.76\%, on par with CryptoTrade full (+8.83\%) and below Buy \& Hold (+9.80\%);
without the test bull period they fall to +3.20\%, below Market only (+5.83\%). The value of the extra agents is
therefore not established: an always-bullish news agent looks useful only in a long rally.

\subsection{Analysis: hidden aggregation bias}
The time-decay penalty treats day-old data as stale: on-chain data and news are from $D{-}1$, so their factor is
$e^{-0.864}\approx0.42$ against 1 for prices. With equal noise, the Market agent therefore carries about 2.5 times the
weight of each other agent, and Sentiment's weight is fixed near $0.5\times0.9\times0.42\approx0.19$. A design choice
intended for live freshness becomes a structural bias in a daily backtest. Signals also do not follow the trend: during
a 40\% rally the system issued 28 SELLs against 19 BUYs, and during the decline 26 BUYs against 24 SELLs, in line with the
biases of Table~\ref{tab:agents}.

\paragraph{Statistical tests.} A paired moving-block bootstrap (blocks of 5 days, 10{,}000 resamples) of the mean
return difference finds no significant difference at the 5\% level: ours minus CryptoTrade full is $-5.33$ points on
test (95\% CI $[-18.1, 3.1]$, $p=0.24$) and $-4.34$ pooled over six ranges ($p=0.24$); the trend filter's test gain of
$+3.33$ has CI $[-2.5, 9.7]$; and no LLM-rerun improvement differs from eval-v1 on dev (debiasing prompts $-6.13$,
$p=0.24$). Exact binomial tests find no accuracy different from 50\%, including CryptoTrade's own final decisions (49.4\%,
$n=253$, $p=0.90$): its higher return comes from exposure, not from better direction calls. Detecting 55\% against 50\%
with 80\% power would need about 780 directional days, three times our data.

\paragraph{Timing.} Shuffling each strategy's own actions within each window (5{,}000 times) separates timing from
exposure. Our shuffled actions earn +0.02\% on average against +4.49\% in the original order, which beats 98.7\% of
shuffles ($p=0.013$); CryptoTrade's shuffles already earn +6.32\% from buying often, and its order adds the rest
($p=0.052$). Although neither system predicts direction at 1--7 days, both action sequences follow multi-week trends
better than chance, so daily directional accuracy alone is a poor measure of a trading agent.

\paragraph{Cost.} Per period, CryptoTrade full needs about 50 minutes and our system about 4.6 hours on the laptop,
because of the RCA, debate and verification calls; the monetary cost of both is zero with a local model.

\section{Conclusion}
Under identical data, environment and model, separating sources into specialised agents and handling their conflict
explicitly does not outperform CryptoTrade's implicit fusion, on the paper's test periods or on 145 held-out days. The
study yields four lessons for conflict-aware agent design. A debate that only decays confidence lowers measured
disagreement without resolving it, and allowing direction changes does not help: the agents almost never change their
mind, and when they do, the minority joins the majority. Individual agents can be near chance and systematically biased, the news agent
mostly by reading upbeat coverage as a buy signal. When agent signals carry little information, aggregation changes such
as regime weights, trend filters and sizing only change market exposure, so their apparent gains depend on the period
evaluated; a dev-then-test protocol exposes this where a single test set would not. Finally, aggregation penalties can
encode unintended biases, and benchmark label conventions can steer an LLM agent against the trend. Telling the agents
about their bias shifts the mix of directions but not their accuracy. Finally, CryptoTrade's edge is exposure, not
prediction, and an arbiter that makes our calls more accurate makes them less profitable: evaluations should separate
exposure from timing and prediction. Future work should present indicators in a standard convention to both systems,
test the reliability arbiter on an unseen asset, and try larger models that may weigh opposing evidence rather than
restate their view.

\section*{Limitations}
Each period has only 35--69 trading days; none of our return or accuracy differences is statistically significant,
so ``trails CryptoTrade'' means ``does not beat it'', not ``is significantly worse''. We study
one asset and one 26B-parameter model; results may differ for ETH, SOL or larger models. The model may have seen 2023
market data during pretraining; this affects both systems equally but may inflate absolute returns. The test set was
used once per selected improvement; we report every such result, but some selection risk remains, and the conflict
analysis and long-run arbiter were explored after the test periods had been seen; no unused dates remain in the dataset. The development set
lacks a sustained bear market. The $\pm0.5$ action mapping and the parameters of the conflict score, debate and
Mediator were set by design intuition. Ablations and aggregation variants are re-aggregations of one run rather than
independent runs.
